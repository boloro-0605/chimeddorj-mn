Talk · 2020 · Erkh Chuluu (Liberty) Movement

The Idea of Liberty: Human Rights, the Limits of the State, and Competition

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · Opening remarks at the Liberty Movement · Full transcript · Updated 2026

Key Ideas

Protection on paper is relative

We tend to think that with the 1992 Constitution we acquired a genuinely democratic constitution safeguarding human rights. But being protected merely by virtue of what is written in a constitution is a relative thing — if we cannot defend our own rights ourselves, those in power will keep making attempts on them.

Two great schools of economics

Studying at the School of Economics of the National University of Mongolia, he encountered economic thought divided into two great branches: the Austrian school and Keynesian economics. The argument between these two bodies of theory, which spread worldwide from the 1930s onward, is what has shaped the economic policy of most countries today.

The deeper foundation of political philosophy

After studying political philosophy at the University of Oxford, his own search began again. What he came to understand is that beneath the foundational philosophies of economics lies something deeper still — the philosophy of human rights. Without grasping those roots, judging what happens in the State Palace or on the street remains far too superficial and one-sided.

A citizen's critical thinking is the real safeguard

If citizens cannot see the processes unfolding in society down to their roots, weigh them, and approach them critically, then the state — being enormously powerful — is capable of destroying an individual's life in the state's own name. The talk notes that the world holds both states that relatively protect human rights and states that use coercion against their own people.

Subsidies destroy competition

The talk criticizes participation in the economy carried out in the state's name with taxpayers' money. It expresses respect for one of the speakers, a business owner whom — by his own account — he was meeting for the first time and knew only by report, for deciding not to take government subsidies and not to back subsidized business. A subsidy, he argues, damages the environment of clean competition in business, and such a decision means a real loss to that individual's own business.

Liberty demands sacrifice

Liberty, the talk concludes, demands a certain sacrifice from all of us. The event brought together speakers including a professor from the Law School of the National University of Mongolia and the director of the Altan Taria company, to discuss how Mongolia's legal system has upheld human rights. It closes by urging young people and students to consider who they themselves must become in order to overcome what confronts them.

Full Transcript

The Liberty Movement presents. First of all, thank you to everyone who has come to this hall. A great many young people have come, and we have broadcast it online. In any case, this hall has been filled.

In the society we live in, we probably think that with the new constitution adopted in 1992 we acquired a genuinely democratic constitution that safeguards human rights and freedoms. And yet in the society we actually live in today, liberty is limited to a certain degree; in some respects there are wrongful actions carried out by the state, and conduct on the part of those in power directed at the individual, in some cases even taking on the character of coercion. In a society where that has become widespread, I believe the event we are organizing today is an important one.

Let me speak briefly about where I found my own political convictions. When I was studying at the School of Economics of the National University of Mongolia, we learned that economic thought divides into two large parts. One of those bodies of ideas is the great branch expressed by the Austrian school of economics; the other is Keynesian economics, which spread across the world from the 1930s onward. The confrontation, the debate and the argument between these two great bodies of theory are what determine the economic policy of most countries today.

So a young person searching for themselves while studying is always looking for where in all this their own convictions lie — and all of you are in that position too, particularly those of you from the Law School. Those of us who are politically active young people are all searching for where, exactly, our own convictions stand.

At the University of Oxford I came to political philosophy and took a course in it. And with that, my own searching began again from the beginning. What I understood from it is that deeper than the foundational philosophies of economics lie the philosophies of human rights — that in political philosophy this is the largest foundation of all. Without understanding those roots, to define ourselves, or to pass judgment on what is happening today in politics, in the State Palace, or on the street and in the media, is something far too superficial and far too one-sided. That is what I came to understand once again.

That is why I am glad that taking part in the event we are organizing today are Professor Ch. Selenge of the Law School of the National University of Mongolia, and Tsengüün, the director of the Altan Taria company — people who have made their own contribution to the thinking, the ideas and the intellectual life of liberty in Mongolia, and who live by that understanding in their own lives. Thank you. And thank you for sharing this with everyone.

Let me say it again: the attitude with which we have judged what unfolds in the society we think we understand looks far too superficial. Unless we ourselves — as citizens, and equally as political parties, and equally as those who have arrived at the powers of state office — can see the roots of the processes unfolding in this society, weigh them and approach them critically, a great many dangerous decisions get made without their consequences being foreseen. And the state, being enormously powerful, turns out to be capable of destroying a person's life at any time in the state's own name. In the world we look at and live in today, some states are ones that relatively protect human rights, while others are states that use coercion against their own people.

So here is the central emphasis I want to give to today's event. The first talk will concern the legal system that exists in Mongolia today — the system of a democratic constitution safeguarding human rights that came into being here — and it will speak to how this legal system of ours has upheld human rights and how it has protected against violations of them. After that, Dr. Dashchuluun will speak on the idea of liberty and the question of human rights.

We sometimes understand things wrongly. For instance, the notion that a person is protected merely because something is written in the constitution, merely because it is written on a piece of paper, is a relative one. Because if we ourselves cannot defend our own rights, the state — and those in power above all — is always making attempts on human rights. And that is present in our society today as well. I think that is another aspect we will be discussing.

As for Tsengüün, the director of the Altan Taria company, speaking as one of our presenters — I am meeting him for the first time today. But from what I know, participation in Mongolia's economy carried out in the name of the state, with taxpayers' money, has gone too far. And I hold in high regard someone who works without taking part in that — who makes the decision not to do business on government subsidies and not to support subsidized business. Because that kind of participation is something that damages the environment of clean competition in business. And not taking it means a very substantial loss is being borne in that person's own business.

So liberty does demand a certain sacrifice from us — in the society we live in right now. On that subject, I expect all of you — our politically active young people, the students, everyone here — will together turn your attention to the foundations of the idea of liberty: to what problems confront us in this society today, and to who we ourselves must become and grow into in order to come through them. Thank you.

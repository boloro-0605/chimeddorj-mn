Financial Discipline and the Crisis of 2016: Positions from a MONTSAME Interview

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · Budget policy, sovereign debt, interest rates

Key Ideas

A structural sensitivity, not just a price shock

Asked how financial discipline is achieved in practice, the interview points past economics to the political system: the electoral system affects budget policy, the independence of government institutions is central to good governance, and the unrealizable welfare promises made during elections need to stop.

What the interview set out

In October 2016 the Mongolian economy was sliding into recession, sovereign debt repayments were coming due, and the government was weighing an IMF Stand-By Arrangement against further credit from China. Chimeddorj Munkhjargal, then an economist and researcher, set out his reading of the situation in an interview with the MONTSAME News Agency, published in English in the Mongol Messenger on 28 October 2016. The account below summarizes the positions he took; the interview itself remains with its publisher.

On the causes of the downturn, he located the problem in the structure of the economy and in budget policy rather than in world prices alone. An economy resting on primary sectors, with mining exports supplying a large share of the state budget, is acutely sensitive to commodity prices; when prices rise the budget expands with them, and the eventual fall does correspondingly greater damage. The slowdown, on his account, came from the drop in world commodity prices on one side and from inaccurate budget policy on the other.

From this followed the central proposition of the interview, and the one the headline took up: financial discipline. When the budget depends heavily on resource prices, expanding it as revenue rises is itself the risk. The budget should not be widened to match incoming revenue; the discipline required is a deficit-free budget. Had that discipline been observed, he argued, the surplus could have accumulated in a sovereign wealth fund capable of sustaining growth over the long term.

On where spending should be cut, he made a distinction that constrains the choice. Increases in social welfare spending cannot realistically be reversed once granted. Budget investment, therefore, should go only to infrastructure genuinely necessary to secure economic returns — and state-funded infrastructure projects should first be ranked by their returns and their importance, so that the most profitable and significant are funded first.

On the debt question he took a clear position. The government had raised substantial sums on international markets while growth and commodity prices were high; policy errors and conditions in the world economy had since eroded trust in it, raising the question of restructuring or deferral. Some argued for taking on further debt and repaying once the economy recovered; others for austerity. His own view was that Mongolia should enter the IMF's Stand-By programme and correct its policy errors. For a resource-based economy, he reasoned, the larger the debt grows the narrower the room to repair domestic policy becomes. He also noted the concern raised by many specialists about Chinese credit: that projects financed this way would largely involve foreign or joint companies and require a substantial foreign workforce, leaving Mongolia with considerably less of the economic benefit.

On the high cost of borrowing, his answer returned to the same principle. Financial discipline is the essential remedy for high interest rates, because an expansionary budget policy feeds through into monetary policy and ends up pushing the policy rate higher.

Asked whether admitting foreign banks — Bank of China was then seeking to open in Mongolia — would introduce competition and bring rates down, he accepted the principle but rejected the timing. Branches of foreign banks can be opened in Mongolia, but that moment had not arrived. Banking institutions then made up 94 percent of Mongolia's financial system, and competitiveness within it was lacking. Two things had to come first: strengthening the capital market, through an accumulation fund able to exert influence on interest rates; and enlarging commercial banks by increasing their capital. Competition among commercial banks sustained on that basis would bring rates down, and the question of foreign banks belonged to a later discussion, once the proper conditions existed.

Asked finally how budget-effective policy and financial discipline are actually to be achieved, he pointed past economics to the political system. The country's electoral system, he acknowledged, makes a difference to budget policy. Improving the independence of government institutions and the principles on which they work is central to good governance. And budget policy has to be freed from populism: the unrealizable social welfare promises made during elections need to stop.

On the Day of Honouring the Victims of the Repressions: Substance and Lessons

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · Four lessons of the Great Repression

Key Ideas

The courts were done away with

In the years of the repressions the courts of Mongolia were abolished, and tens of thousands were put to death by decisions of a body called the Extraordinary Plenipotentiary Commission. The mass arrests of 10 September 1937 began without a prosecutor's authorization, and that commission was set up in October outside the court system.

First lesson: checks and balances

Where there is no right to be tried by a fair court, there are no human rights and no freedoms. Building and defending the mutual checks and balances of the legislative, executive and judicial powers therefore remains the central challenge for this generation and the next.

Second lesson: the death penalty

Tens of thousands were killed under sentences imposed without trial on fabricated charges; by the time this became clear, the harm could no longer be reduced. At no time, the article argues, should the Mongolian state use the death penalty — if the state errs, the victim of that error should at least have been left their life.

Third lesson: sovereignty

The institutions of the Mongolian state not only failed to stop what was happening but took part in it as direct executors. The true initiator, organizer and beneficiary, the article states, was a force lying beyond Mongolia's borders.

What independence actually rests on

Independence does not rest on borders and territorial integrity alone; it depends on the inviolability of state institutions and their capacity to reach their own decisions — on sovereignty. Without strengthening that capacity, the risk of being overpowered by an external force remains, now and in future.

The question of terminology

Sentences imposed outside the courts may be called repression. But given the deliberate killing of tens of thousands, the use of armed force against civilians, the destruction of particular groups of the population, and the grave damage to the nation, the article concludes this must be regarded as a crime against humanity and as genocide — and that screening it behind the mild word "repression" does not accord with historical justice.

The Article

Ardent faith driven to despair,
steadfast hearts left wounded —
those years of nightmare:
let no Mongol ever forget them.

Today falls the day of honouring the memory of the victims of the repressions. To recall what we must take from the dark years our forebears lived through — the years known as the Great Repression — and why the generations coming after us must not forget, let me single out four things.

1. Human rights: the courts

In the years of the repressions the courts of Mongolia were done away with, and tens of thousands of people were put to death by the decisions of a body called the Extraordinary Plenipotentiary Commission. The mass arrests of 10 September 1937 began without any authorization from a prosecutor, and in October that commission was established outside the court system altogether.

Lesson: Where there is no right to be tried by a fair court, there are no human rights and no freedoms. That is precisely why building and defending the mutual checks and balances of the legislative, executive and judicial powers remains the central challenge — and the most important task — both for our own generation and for those to come.

2. Human rights: the death penalty

In the years of the repressions, tens of thousands were put to death under sentences imposed without trial and on false charges. Although this later became clear, by then it had been made impossible to undo it, or even to reduce the harm. A life once taken cannot be restored by anything at all.

Lesson: At no time should the Mongolian state make use of the death penalty. This is not merely an achievement of humanity's social progress, but a bitter lesson of the history Mongols have lived through: if the state errs, then at the very least the victim of that error ought to have been left their life. Today's democratic Mongolian state has no right to end a citizen's life in the name of the law, and must not have that right in future either.

3. Independence: sovereignty

Not only did the institutions of the Mongolian state fail to halt what unfolded at the time — they were participants in it, and its direct executors. Seen from one side it may appear that Mongols destroyed one another; but the true initiator, the organizer, and the one that profited from the outcome was a force lying beyond Mongolia's borders. That the Mongolian state could not protect itself or its own citizens before a force driven by foreign political and military-strategic interests is a profound tragedy.

Lesson: Unless we can consolidate and strengthen the Mongolian state's capacity to make its own decisions, and its inviolability, the risk of being overpowered before an external force exists today and will exist in the future. Independence does not rest on borders and territorial integrity alone; it depends on the inviolability of state institutions and on their capacity to reach their own decisions — on sovereignty, to put it precisely.

Finally, a question of terminology: repression, or mass killing?

Sentences imposed outside the courts, on fabricated charges, may indeed be described as sentences of repression. But when one considers that tens of thousands of people were killed deliberately, that armed force was used against civilians, that particular groups of the population were destroyed — political figures, descendants of the Golden Lineage, Buddhist monks, ethnic groups and others — and that this reached a scale inflicting grave damage on the share of the population and on the nation's hereditary stock, then this is to be regarded as a crime against humanity and as genocide.

Lesson: Be forever on guard against politicians, political forces and external forces that divide a population into camps and conduct propaganda and political activity in a register of hatred. And henceforth, to screen what took place — the Mongols who lost their lives and their livelihoods, the harm done to the nation — behind the mild word "repression" does not accord with historical justice. That is why it would greatly help future generations to grasp the truth if scholars of the social sciences and progressive thinkers would settle, through careful reflection, what terminology genuinely expresses the truth. For my own part, I understand these events as a state that had lost its sovereignty falling under an alien force and taking part, by Mongol hands, in the destruction of Mongols as a people.

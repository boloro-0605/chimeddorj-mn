Free Economy: Competition Is Its Lifeblood

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · On the free economy · Full transcript · Updated 2026

Key Ideas

Honest work no longer guarantees a living

Through the stories of friends whose bakery went bankrupt, who could never win a tender, and who fly abroad by a roundabout route because domestic airfares are too expensive, the talk shows how scarce the chance has become today for people to build a life with their own two hands.

The difference between evolution and revolution

Gradual, everyday change is evolution, while a deep, qualitative change that occurs within a short span of time is revolution. The talk explains why the changes of 1990 and 1992 amounted to a genuine revolution.

The two qualitative shifts of the 1992 Constitution

It treats two great transitions as the foundation of the democratic Constitution: from a socialist system to a democratic polity that upholds human rights, and from a centrally planned economy to a market economic system.

Property rights mean the chance to build a life

It puts forward a broader understanding of property rights — not merely owning a car or a house, but giving every person the chance to build their own life with their own two hands.

Did we simply inherit a competition-free economy?

Using the idea behind the word "derivative," the talk raises a doubt: if today's society has simply inherited the competition-free economy that existed before 1990, can that really be called a genuine revolution?

In a society without competition, people compete for state power

When there is no competition, businesses stop competing with one another and instead compete for state power, favorable loans, licenses, and access to corruption at state-owned companies. The talk calls for restoring competition through Adam Smith's "invisible hand."

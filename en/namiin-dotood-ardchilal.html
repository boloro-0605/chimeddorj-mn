Inner-Party Democracy: Rules, Money, and Pluralism

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · On inner-party democracy · Full transcript · Updated 2026

Key Ideas

Defeat as a window for reform

Political parties only tend to reform and improve their internal governance after losing an election — the DP's 2016 defeat opened the door to three targeted reforms: ideology, rules, and accountability.

A party's core job is screening talent

A political party is not merely an institution built to win elections: it is a system for identifying and preparing the best personnel to compete in state elections through genuine competition.

Owning up to a mistake in the charter

A chairman's election open to all members with no prior screening produced a leader with only about 30% support and weak legitimacy, and the confidence-vote accountability mechanism disappeared from the final charter — a mistake he attributes directly to his own group.

Oversight bodies lost their independence

The party's "audit, court, and election commission" — the Charter Committee and the Internal Election Committee — became dependent once their members were confirmed on the chairman's recommendation, becoming a major obstacle to internal reform.

Pluralism — the living spirit of the Constitution

A provision allowing the expulsion of members who voice views at odds with the leadership contradicts the principle of tolerating views one may dislike; unity should mean agreement on ideology and principle, not obedience.

Clean, money-free internal governance pulls society forward

Money entering internal party elections distorts a party away from its core purpose; the way out is to cut costs, be honest with the public, and pursue policy-based politics — in a democracy, a party's internal governance should lead society, not follow it.

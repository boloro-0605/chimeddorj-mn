Fast-Track Lawmaking, a Twenty-Year Fuel Contract, and the EAEU: Positions from 2025

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · Four policy positions, given in his capacity as a researcher

Key Ideas

Urgent procedure and the broken chain of accountability

Unless the government is restrained from submitting matters under urgent procedure, members cannot study them in advance and decide on insufficient information. If the decision then proves wrong, the government that submitted the bill can no longer be held to account — which creates an interest in avoiding responsibility.

A contract locked for twenty years

The aviation fuel draft would create a monopoly supplier, supply a single brand of fuel for twenty years, stand above Mongolia's own legislation and its prior international agreements, and send disputes to foreign arbitration. Over twenty years technology, markets, infrastructure and even geopolitics change — so an unalterable contract, he concluded, is not in Mongolia's interest.

Standards, carbon charges, and the biofuel option

Countries are beginning to levy greenhouse-gas charges on air tickets, yet the fuel to be contracted does not meet international standards. Mongolia is meanwhile spending heavily on its own oil refinery while holding the resources to produce biofuel.

The EAEU: 24 to 1, and the ministry's own projection

Trade with the northern neighbour is skewed 24 to 1, with fuel dominating imports. By the Ministry of Economy and Development's own calculation, an interim agreement would worsen rather than improve the balance, treble imports and reduce tax revenue — the modelling pointing the opposite way from the agreement's stated purpose.

The arithmetic of flour and lamb

Although wheat and flour tariffs are not to be zeroed, cheaper flour-based products risk contracting the domestic flour market. On meat, union members are themselves lamb exporters, while Mongolia's sheep are three to five years old — suggesting the problem may lie in quality rather than tariffs.

Exchange versus barter

A price set openly by bid on an exchange is the market price, and it removes the margin in which corruption sits. A clause allowing coal to be sold by barter would mean mining products ceasing to be expressed in money, with no clear answer as to how tax is collected. The earlier offtake contracts, still classified, should be opened first.

What the interview set out

In an interview given to inews.mn on 25 April 2025, Chimeddorj Munkhjargal set out his position on four questions then before parliament carrying economic and security consequences. He specified that he answered not in his capacity as senior adviser to the Democratic Party caucus in the State Great Khural, but as a researcher. What follows summarizes the policy substance of that conversation.

The first question concerned legislative procedure. Unless the government is restrained from submitting matters to parliament under urgent procedure, he argued, members are left unable to study them in advance, and the conditions are created for decisions taken on insufficient information. Hence the criticism now heard that parliament has turned into an office that stamps whatever the government sends it. Beneath this lies a broken chain of accountability: if members decide wrongly because they were under-informed, the government that submitted the bill can no longer be held to account — which gives the government an interest in avoiding responsibility on certain matters by having them passed under urgent procedure.

The second was a draft twenty-year agreement on the supply of aviation fuel. Mongolia is one of the few landlocked countries, and weak infrastructure leaves its products uncompetitive — but its air corridor is of particular importance: a potential transit point linking Asia and Europe, positioned to carry not only passengers but light, high-value cargo, with a security dimension besides. The draft, however, would establish a monopoly supplier jointly with the largest supplier of the day, supplying a single brand of fuel for twenty years, holding a standing above Mongolia's own legislation and the international agreements it has previously concluded, with any dispute settled by foreign arbitration. In short, he concluded, a contract that cannot be altered for twenty years.

Why is twenty years the wrong horizon? Over that span, technology, markets, the structure of the economy, infrastructure — even geopolitics and international relations — will change. He gave concrete instances: Mongolia is working to acquire long-haul flights, and countries are beginning to levy greenhouse-gas charges on air tickets, yet the fuel to be contracted does not meet international standards. Mongolia is also spending heavily on its own oil refinery while holding the resources to produce biofuel. On the constitutional side he singled out one thing: the draft agreement laid before parliament was a translation bearing the foreign ministry's official stamp, and the government should not treat parliament in that way.

The third was an interim agreement with the Eurasian Economic Union. While accepting that trade with both neighbours ought to be expanded, he opposed joining the union as a member. The northern neighbour applies the union's rules to questions of trade and customs; when the subject is lowering tariffs, accession is raised and pressed, and a country that does not join is left proceeding as though under tariff embargo. The trade balance is skewed at a ratio of 24 to 1, and fuel and petroleum products make up most of the imports. Even so, he held, membership is not acceptable: decisions in the union are dominated by Russia, and complaints from member states — over trade deficits, labour migration, shifts in customs revenue and investment — never cease.

He stressed that the projection for the interim agreement itself points the other way: by the Ministry of Economy and Development's own calculation, concluding it would worsen rather than improve the trade balance, leaving Mongolia importing three times what it does now, with consumption rising as a share of GDP and tax revenue falling. Doubts arise at the level of actual goods too. The war has left a large surplus of below-market wheat accumulated in Belarus and Russia; and although tariffs on wheat and flour are not to be zeroed, every flour-based product — spirits, noodles, pastry and the rest — becomes cheaper. Since most domestic flour consumption goes precisely into those manufactures, the domestic flour market risks contracting, and with it the land under wheat. On meat, union members Russia, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan are themselves exporters of lamb to Iran and the Gulf states, while the sheep Mongolia prepares are three to five years old and thus not lamb at all. Had quality requirements been met, a kilogram of lamb could be sold to Iran or the Gulf at four to six dollars even by air — so the question of whether the problem lies in tariffs or in quality is being left undecided while the agreement proceeds. And since businesses do not invest against a three-year window, treating an interim agreement as an opportunity would be a frivolous expectation.

The fourth was the law on the mining exchange. An exchange is a place that sets prices, and a price set on an exchange is the market price; trading openly by bid removes the margin in which corruption sits. That coal prices are falling, buyers fewer and volumes smaller does not mean an exchange is unnecessary — one does not wind up the New York exchange every time there is a financial crisis. What is most serious is a clause admitted into the draft allowing coal to be sold by barter. Barter means mining products cease to be expressed in money — a form of exchange older than money itself, of the order of handing over a million tonnes of coal in return for a hundred kilometres of railway. From such a contract, the question of how tax is to be collected at all arises immediately.

He tied the barter question to what came before: offtake contracts worth billions of dollars were concluded and then classified in their entirety as state secrets, done after the Law on State and Official Secrets was rewritten in the autumn of 2016, so that they can be neither inspected nor seen by the public. Anyone genuinely fighting theft, he argued, ought to begin by lifting the secrecy on those contracts and not repeating the old mistake — and instead a barter clause has arrived. He illustrated the consequences with concrete cases: an oil pipeline laid toward a field where nothing was found, a railway with nothing to carry, a processing plant standing unused — none of which passed through parliament or appeared in the budget, and all of which he judged the product of mega-projects beyond the country's economic capacity, undertaken without calculation or study.

He closed by marking the boundaries of his own role. Approaching questions bearing on the country's fate and development in depth — reading, studying and reaching conclusions — is, he said, the moral duty of the path he has chosen; drawing conclusions within the frame of the Democratic Party's principles and informing the caucus is the duty of his job; and not intruding on members' authority to decide is the principle he works by. The positions he expresses on social media, he added, are not an obligation of that job but should be understood as the expression of his own political will.

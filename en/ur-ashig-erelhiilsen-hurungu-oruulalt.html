Efficiency-Seeking Investment: Low Taxes Alone Will Not Attract It

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · The four types of FDI and Mongolia's composition

Key Ideas

Four types of foreign investment

Foreign direct investment is studied under four headings — resource-seeking, market-seeking, efficiency-seeking and strategic-asset-seeking. The classification is decisive for knowing which type a policy is aimed at. Investment flowing to developing countries makes up some 40 percent of the world total: 1.75 trillion US dollars as of 2017.

Mongolia's composition: 81 percent into resources

Of all investment made between 2012 and 2016, 81 percent was resource-seeking, 16 percent market-seeking and three percent strategic-capability-seeking. Efficiency-seeking investment was almost entirely absent — and without attracting the other types, the economy cannot be diversified.

Low taxes are not themselves a draw

Research confirms that efficiency-seeking investors weigh macroeconomic and exchange-rate stability more heavily than other investors do, and that the adequacy of infrastructure is decisive in their decisions. Low taxes on their own, therefore, will not attract them.

The cost of abrupt legal change

Sudden changes in the legal framework fall hardest on those who have already invested: trust weakens, and decisions follow about freezing investment or closing the business. Because foreign investors weigh the experience of earlier investors, the damage reaches future decisions too.

74th on Doing Business, 148th on electricity

The weakness of infrastructure was put in figures: Mongolia stood 74th of 190 countries on the Doing Business index, yet 148th on the ability to connect to the electricity system. Transport cost per tonne-kilometre to a seaport is also high compared with other landlocked countries.

State-owned versus publicly listed investors

Foreign state-owned companies show a tendency to take non-commercial decisions in order to gain advantage in a market. Other countries' experience, by contrast, shows that investment by publicly listed companies tends to be more accountable and to strengthen good governance.

What the notes set out

On 20 December 2018 the Ulaanbaatar Analytics development research institute, together with the Institute for Security Studies, held a policy discussion on trends in foreign direct investment directed at developing countries, moderated by Chimeddorj Munkhjargal. He wrote up its findings as a set of notes published by ikon.mn; what follows summarizes their substance.

The discussion opened on the global picture. Foreign investment flowing into developing countries accounts for some 40 percent of all investment worldwide — 1.75 trillion US dollars as of 2017. Beyond improving the general business environment, countries have come to design and implement distinct policies aimed at each separate type of investment.

That distinction begins with a taxonomy. Foreign direct investment is studied under four headings: resource-seeking, market-seeking, efficiency-seeking, and strategic-asset or capability-seeking. The classification is not a theoretical nicety — it is decisive for knowing which type a policy is actually aimed at. In countries that have created conditions favourable to efficiency-seeking investment, investment has risen across many sectors of the economy and diversification has benefited.

Mongolia's own composition then becomes legible. Of all investment made between 2012 and 2016, 81 percent was resource-seeking, 16 percent market-seeking, and three percent strategic-asset or capability-seeking. Efficiency-seeking investment, on the other hand, can be regarded as almost entirely absent. Resource-seeking investment makes an important contribution to development, but without attracting the other types the economy cannot be diversified.

So what do efficiency-seekers actually look at? Research confirms that, compared with other investors, they place greater weight on macroeconomic stability and exchange-rate stability, and that the adequacy of infrastructure is a decisive factor in their decisions. From this follows the conclusion the headline took up: low taxes on their own will not attract efficiency-seeking investors.

The general state of the business environment is the baseline every type of investor weighs — the stability of the legal framework, the clarity of regulation, and the transparency of state bodies across every stage of a business: starting up, operating, repatriating dividends, and exiting. Abrupt changes in law fall hardest on those who have already invested: trust weakens, and unwelcome decisions follow about whether to freeze planned investment or shut the business down. Since foreign investors studying a country's environment attach weight to the experience of those who invested before them, this affects not only present decisions but future ones.

Uncertainty and opacity were identified as causing two distinct harms: regulatory uncertainty creates the conditions in which state bodies treat businesses unequally and at their own discretion, while opacity creates the conditions for abrupt decisions that hand an advantage to a competing business. And because information on ownership disputes and how they were resolved is scarce, prospective investors continue to make decisions on the basis of second-hand report.

The weakness of infrastructure was put in figures, and these made the sharpest illustration: Mongolia stood 74th of 190 countries on the Doing Business index, yet 148th on the ability to connect to the electricity system. The cost of transport per tonne-kilometre to a seaport is also high compared with other landlocked countries.

The policy recommendations arising from the discussion: alongside increasing investment in the minerals sector, design policy that encourages investment in non-mineral sectors; incorporate the views of investors and the private sector whenever the legal framework is changed; study and draw conclusions on what produced the public mood against foreign investment in 2009–2011; and increase the electricity supply while improving the efficiency of transport and logistics. The notes also observe that foreign state-owned companies show a tendency to take non-commercial decisions in order to gain advantage in a market, whereas other countries' experience shows that investment by publicly listed companies tends to be more accountable and to strengthen good governance.

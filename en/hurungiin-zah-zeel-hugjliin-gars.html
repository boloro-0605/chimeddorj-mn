A Way Out Through the Capital Market: Interest Rates, Accumulation, Twenty Years in Education

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · Accumulation, the two wings of finance, the knowledge economy

Key Ideas

GDP does not measure development in full

Unemployment, GDP per head and growth rates do not fully express social development — a point on which many countries now agree, turning instead toward inclusive growth, balance and long-run sustainability. Placed on that ladder, Mongolia still rests on its primary sector.

The cashmere paradox

Mongolia supplies more than thirty percent of the world's raw cashmere on its own, yet its finished product has still not become an internationally recognized brand. Exporting raw material with only first-stage processing leaves the economy swinging with every movement in world prices.

Accumulation matters more than import substitution

Most see import substitution as the way out, but in theoretical terms it is the wrong instinct. Building accumulation is sounder than building clusters or substituting imports — and the sectors that can genuinely be developed must be identified correctly first.

A 17.3 percent rate and what lies beneath it

Mongolia's real lending rate averaged 17.3 percent in 2015, against five percent in China, six in Russia and around one in South Korea and the United States. The root cause is the budget deficit: as the government issues bonds into circulation, the central bank raises its policy rate, leaving it unable to run an independent monetary policy.

The missing pillar: the stock exchange

With banks holding 94 percent of the financial market, they face no competitor and the only source of business investment is the high-interest loan. Had the budget been held to 30 percent of GDP between 2007 and 2015, 4.3 trillion tögrög could have accumulated and given the capital market real turnover.

Twenty years in education, and the value of one person

Building a knowledge-based economy requires an entire education system, and raising one takes twenty years at the very least. Because Mongolia is short of labour, a single Mongolian is of high economic value — and can compete only by being highly productive and responsible.

What the interview set out

gogo.mn's "Leaderships forum" column invited leading figures from each field to discuss Mongolia's paths to development. On 5 December 2016 the guest was Chimeddorj Munkhjargal, who set out his views on the stages of development, the financial system, and long-horizon investment in education. What follows summarizes the substance of that conversation.

His opening move concerned measurement. Development has conventionally been gauged by unemployment, GDP per head and the rate of economic growth, yet many countries have come to agree that such indicators do not fully express social development, and have gone looking for new indices. Inclusive growth, balance, and long-run sustainability have moved to the centre instead. Placed on that ladder, Mongolia still rests on its primary sector; the next rung is an industrial economy — the structure that ran from the late nineteenth century to the 1980s — and above it the service and knowledge economies into which Japan, Germany, the United States, South Korea and the European countries have already moved.

The clearest evidence that Mongolia has not industrialized is cashmere. The country supplies, on its own, more than thirty percent of the world's raw cashmere, and yet its finished product has still not become a brand recognized internationally. A structure that exports agricultural raw material with only first-stage processing leaves the economy swinging with every movement in world prices.

Most people see import substitution as the way out, but in theoretical terms he regarded that as the wrong instinct. What is needed first is correctly identifying which sectors can actually be developed — a scholar from Harvard who came to Mongolia in 2008, listing the possibilities, named tourism, information technology, meat production, cashmere and mining. What has been missing is any stable programme to develop them. What arrived instead was the resource curse, made real: the surge in commodity prices distorted the thinking of those at the level where decisions are made, and that distortion is its first symptom.

The consequence landed in the budget. While growth was high, the country imagined it would develop along the lines of the Arab oil states, forgot how far it depended on commodity prices, widened its spending, acquired a budget beyond its own capacity, and let the state involve itself in everything — which opened the door to the next crisis. The following year's budget was approved with a nine percent deficit; foreign loans had been taken and the budget widened, yet by the time repayment came due revenue had fallen away. The first thing to do, he said, is to know the size of one's own blanket.

Expressed in figures, the pressing problem was the cost of credit. In 2015 Mongolia's real lending rate averaged 17.3 percent. At that same moment the figure stood at five percent in China, six in Russia, around one in South Korea and the United States, and in Japan and Britain had gone negative. Against that spread, nothing produced in Mongolia can compete. The root cause is the budget deficit: to cover it the government issues bonds in volume and puts the proceeds into circulation, and the central bank has no choice but to raise its policy rate in response. State profligacy, in other words, has left the central bank unable to run an independent monetary policy, weakening macroeconomic management.

Here the idea that gave the conversation its name appears: one central pillar is missing from Mongolia's economy — the stock exchange. With banks holding 94 percent of the financial market in their own hands, they face no competitor, and the sole source of investment for business has become the high-interest bank loan. Companies consequently cannot modernize production or raise wages; they get through the days trying not to close their doors. Had the state budget been held to 30 percent of GDP between 2007 and 2015, he calculated, 4.3 trillion tögrög could have accumulated, and had that gone into the capital market the market would have had real turnover. Rates will fall only when both wings of the financial market develop together.

The nearest channel for accumulation is the social insurance fund. The pension insurance fund alone had run a deficit of 392 billion tögrög in the previous year and more than 400 billion in that one — equivalent to two percent of GDP, and this at the most favourable demographic moment the country will see. The first step is for the government to settle the debt it owes that fund, build accumulation from it and place it on the exchange, and in time establish a national accumulation fund. Do that, and the financial market stops depending on banks alone, while the sovereign credit rating rises of its own accord.

On industry he framed a choice. With a major manufacturing country next door, competing on world markets with finished goods is not realistic; the idea of import substitution should be abandoned; and if the industrial road is taken it means moving into metallurgy and chemicals, or else becoming a supplier of one part within the large industrial complexes of the world. But industry, in truth, is not the point in itself — training people to design a single component of a car, for instance, is worth more. Building a knowledge-based economy requires an entire education system, and raising one takes twenty years at the very least.

He closed on people. After the 1992 Constitution the package of laws defining the business environment was actively passed through to 1998, but since then, on his assessment, nothing substantial has been done in the social sphere. Education is among the factors that create the long-run differences between populations; and because Mongolia is one of the countries short of labour, a single Mongolian ought to be of high economic value compared with countries of many millions — that person can compete only by being highly productive and responsible. His conclusion: development is carried not by politicians but by a private sector that is enterprising, industrious and disciplined, and that seeks profit.

The Coal Theft That Could Even Bring Down Parliamentary Government

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · What must be investigated behind the coal theft

The Article

In Mongolia today, cases of corruption and abuse of office are counted in tens of trillions. Put in terms of the Mongolian names for numbers, adding order upon order, we have reached a figure so vast it runs to ten to the thirteenth power. The coal theft now causing such an uproar in society is only one part of the corruption, embezzlement and theft that have spread so widely in Mongolia. This great theft, tangled in on itself, this plundering and stripping of the country, testifies as a whole to the disintegration of the Mongolian state. Here I will speak only of the coal theft that has shaken society in recent days.

Behind the overly general label "coal theft" lie many separate cases of corruption and theft. To treat it in such general terms, as though it were a single theft, does not correspond to reality and will yield nothing. Results will come only if each theft is taken separately, the relevant information is made public, and accountability is demanded.

To set out one by one the matters behind the name "coal theft", at the very least:

The coal contracts

The coal sales contracts of Erdenes Tavan Tolgoi JSC have remained closed to the public to this day. Although by law they ought to be open, nobody knows which companies are selected by which criteria to conclude coal sales contracts. It is said that from after the 2016 election, coal sales contracts came to be concluded at the top leadership level of the ruling party with the approval of the Cabinet Secretariat. If these contracts were made public, it would become clear which companies bought how much coal, when, and at what price. So the first step is to open up every coal contract concluded by Erdenes Tavan Tolgoi JSC between 2016 and 2022. Only then will we learn who the shareholders of the contracting companies are and which politicians they are connected to, and also how far the prices diverged from the market price of the time. Beyond that, the officials who signed these contracts must explain on what decision and by what criteria they concluded them. From here the names of politicians and state officials will follow one after another, and the major coal thieves will become identifiable.

The dispute over the mining face

The dispute over the vanished mining face is a dispute between Erdenes Tavan Tolgoi JSC and Tavantolgoi JSC, owned by Umnugovi province, in which one company extracted coal from the other's area. Naturally, a dispute over mine workings between two companies will be settled finally by the courts on the basis of expert bodies' assessments. A dispute of this kind is resolved within the framework of civil law. If one party extracted more coal than the other, then it must pay the other the price at which it sold that coal — or the price at which it could have been sold at the time — less extraction costs such as overburden removal. But if there is an official who deliberately created the opportunity for the other company to extract coal and drew a benefit from it, whether directly or through a related party, then it accords with justice that criminal liability be imposed on that official.

Intermediaries in coal sales

It is held that there are intermediary companies in coal sales — companies existing only on paper, which use state power and political influence to acquire coal far below the market price and then sell it on beyond the border at a different price. This matter is again bound up with the transparency of the coal contracts. Erdenes Tavan Tolgoi and the locally owned Tavantolgoi JSC are public companies, and all their operations ought to be transparent and open. So every contract these companies have concluded since 2005 should be made public. Once the coal sales contracts are open, the buyers and the prices become open. If this is examined, the names of many people currently holding state power will come to light.

Unregistered coal exports

Unregistered coal exports are the largest problem of all. Unregistered coal means, precisely, the difference between the volume recorded as exported on the Mongolian side and the volume received on the Chinese side. Specifically, Member of Parliament Dorjkhand has stated that between 2013 and 2021 there were 6.4 million tonnes that went unregistered on the Mongolian side yet were recorded as imports arriving on the Chinese side. For coal to have left unregistered in that volume over eight years is entirely possible.

The talk of a 40-trillion coal theft is an estimate derived solely from this discussion of unregistered exports. If it is true, then a crime of such magnitude, committed over such a long period, must have been carried out with a high degree of organization. To be specific, theft on that scale would be impossible without the involvement of the Border Protection, Intelligence, Police, Customs and Tax authorities. This is the matter that must be investigated first, and the one that will be hardest to investigate.

The corruption surrounding coal transport

The corruption surrounding coal transport became a disaster from 2021 onward. With border controls tightened on the Chinese side and the coal price passing 400 dollars, a corruption race unfolded over who would get their coal across the border — that is, a race to sell coal bought at 70 dollars for 400. At the Tsagaan Khad customs loading and unloading yard, which sits just short of the Gashuunsukhait border port, 11 million tonnes of coal had accumulated at the start of this year. Between March and May 2022 the price for hauling one tonne of coal 20 kilometres from that yard and unloading it beyond the border reached 135 dollars. For a single haul by a 100-tonne truck that means 13,500 dollars, or 50 million tögrög — meaning that a newly bought truck earns back its own price in just three border crossings. Here, who gets their coal out came down in truth to who has influence over the state and over politics, and who will pay how much in bribes. The upshot was a quarantine regime of so-called Covid isolation bubbles, in which some 4,500 people awaiting their turn to haul were confined in isolation quarters. There are reports that bribes of 30 million tögrög were paid there simply to have a Covid test returned positive. All of this must be investigated. On top of this, a rule was issued that only container transport would be permitted, so that a handful of better-funded companies bought trucks and took coal across the border.

The right to haul coal across the border likewise came to be granted by an informal structure called the "port council", set up by the Cabinet Secretariat. So if one traces who belongs to this port council that issues permits to companies taking coal across the border, and whose companies' coal is being exported in greater volume, the owners of the transport corruption become all but identifiable.

The railway bartered for coal

The next grave matter is the contracts to build a railway on terms of taking payment in coal — the ones being called Offtake. In truth they are not Offtake contracts at all but barter contracts. These contracts have been classified in their entirety as state secrets. Neither selling coal nor building a railway is a matter that falls under state secrecy. These are trade and construction works unfolding before the eyes of the whole population.

The main things to be examined in these contracts classified as secret are: at what price the coal was valued, and how many tonnes were given to which company. On the other side of it, at what cost the railway is being built, which companies are taking part in the construction work, and what goods, works and services were supplied at what prices — all of this needs to be made public. In principle, for a company carrying out construction work won through open competitive tender, the prices at which it buys materials and the contracts under which it takes on subcontractors are that company's own business. But because this work was done under a state-secret classification, the likelihood is high that politicians and holders of state power extracted money through their own companies, and it is therefore a matter that simply must be investigated.

Another grave matter that must be raised here is the involvement of military units in the Offtake coal contracts and the railway construction. This too must be investigated. It must be established what expenditures were made under the guise of involving soldiers in construction, and who received the profit from it. Going forward, drawing the armed forces — and the commanders of military units in particular — into money and financial dealings is itself a matter that may bring grave harm to national security. This is precisely why Myanmar cannot free itself from the regime of its military junta.

The matters set out above, surrounding the coal theft, are the ones most closely connected to those currently in power. Some of these decisions relating to coal trade and export were implemented by decisions of the National Security Council and of the Cabinet Secretariat. If we are to investigate and establish this lawfully, the senior state officials who were making those decisions must first be removed from office.

Judging by the recent course of the demonstrations, an initiative to dissolve Parliament keeps surfacing. If Parliament is dissolved, there will be no subject with the legal competence to annul a presidential decision, and the country will pass into disorder. What is therefore needed, and urgently, is to remove the government, appoint the next government, and make public all the information surrounding the coal theft. Above all, no one who aspires to take full control of state power should be given the chance to use the coal theft as a weapon for cutting down their political rivals.

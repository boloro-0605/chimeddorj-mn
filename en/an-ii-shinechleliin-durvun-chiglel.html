Interview · 2016 · Eagle News "Guest Hour"

Reforming the Democratic Party: Ideology, Participation, and the Money in Internal Elections

Chimeddorj Munkhjargal · A political interview · Full transcript · Updated 2026

Key Ideas

A party must first define the society it wants

Before reform can be discussed, one has to consider what role a political party plays in society. A modern party's ideology rests on how social institutions — business, the state, civil society — ought to be organized. Since winning an election means steering state power, a party has to articulate first how it sees society.

Four directions of reform

A personal framework set out in four parts: first, whether the ideas of the 1990 democratic revolution were actually inherited by today's party; second, whether the party has the institutions and the people to formulate policy; third, whether accountability works from the ordinary member up to the leadership; fourth, whether decisions are made with genuine member participation. Only taken together do these amount to reform.

The price of a mechanical merger

After the 2000 defeat the opposition forces of the day were merged to found today's party. But because it was too mechanical a merger aimed at winning the next election, parties with differing ideologies never arrived at a shared understanding of what the new party actually stood for — something visible, the interview argues, in the economic policy that followed.

Where factions came from

Factions emerged inside the party once rotation on the National Advisory Committee stopped: with members no longer being replaced or re-elected, whoever could count a majority among the sitting members could carry their own proposals. Seven years on, the interview notes, the people elected back then were still in place.

Why money flows into internal elections

A party's real expenditure falls at election time, yet a great deal of money goes into internal elections. The cause is the mechanism of delegates electing delegates: because National Advisory Committee members are chosen by district party committees of roughly 100–105 people, buying that number of people becomes feasible and power shifts upward.

The way out: elect from the primary level

If National Advisory Committee members and the party chair were elected directly by the primary-level membership — establishing the principle that representatives must not elect representatives — internal elections would no longer need large sums and buying power would cease to be possible. Alongside this: reform the election law to cut campaign costs, digitize the membership register, and govern admission by the charter rather than by an official's discretion.

Full Transcript

Thank you again to all our viewers for your attention. We are continuing our program with the guest hour. This time we have in our studio a member of the coordinating council of the Open Democratic Party Reform Movement, the economist Chimeddorj. Thank you very much for accepting our invitation and coming in. Thank you.

We are working today with a particular focus on the subject of Democratic Party reform, in order to give our viewers information on it. Do you consider that the party needs reform? Yes. First of all, before we talk about reform, I think we have to begin by considering what role a political party actually performs in society.

The ideology of a modern political party is anchored, first and foremost, on the question of how this society ought to be organized — on which arrangement of social institutions is the right one. Within that frame we ask, for instance: what role do business organizations perform in society, and in what environment should they operate? In what form should the institutions of the state be organized, and how should they work? How should civil society organizations work? Political parties compete, first of all, with their views on how the institutions of this society ought to be organized. And because a political party that wins an election ultimately acquires the right to steer state power, it has to define first of all how it sees this society.

So this is not only a question about reforming the Democratic Party. The role that the political parties in our society perform, and what their vision is, is a crucial question in itself. Second, the Democratic Party is unquestionably the major political force that has led social change and reform in Mongolia, especially in the period since the democratic revolution. Even though it was defeated in this election, in terms of ideology and the scope of its work the Democratic Party occupies a very large place in Mongolian society. For that reason its renewal is inevitable.

In the open social order of today, if a political party proves unable to renew itself, then it inevitably loses its own space in this society and another political force emerges to take its place. That is why I understand reform of the Democratic Party as not being the Democratic Party's own question alone.

An Open Democratic Party Reform Movement has been organized, yes. When people ask today whether the Democratic Party has genuinely renewed itself or not, it turns out young people have created a great many reform movements here and there. First, "Open Democratic Party" — your movement. Its main aim? And within that aim there must, of course, be objectives.

Yes. Change and reform — especially in large organic social systems, in organizations as structured as a political party — depend on how open the thing itself is. For instance: is there a problem with joining the Democratic Party as a member, or not? Second, what rights does that member have, and does the member genuinely have a part in that organization, or not? How does the internal election system work; how do members take part in decisions connected with ideology? It depends a great deal on whether it is open or not.

Why was this Open Democratic Party Reform Movement founded over the past period? Because it is a voluntary organization founded by young people of the Democratic Party who came together on the view that this party is not open — and there are a great many such organizations. Its distinguishing feature is that this organization's main aim is to secure the participation of the party's own members. That is the central issue. But if reform of a political party is confined to a few people inside it, that reform will not happen. Which is why I understand that new air, new people and new ideas coming in matters enormously.

Looking at all these reform movements — do you see it this way? Altankhuyag's faction, the youth; Erdene's faction; Democracy's Youth; Shonkhor's young people; and so the factions each seem to be raising up their own young people from below. What do you make of that? Am I seeing it wrongly?

Yes, well. Where did this concept of a faction in the Democratic Party come from? It came about once internal rotation in the party stopped. Because members of the National Advisory Committee were no longer being replaced or re-elected, a system emerged of counting the numbers of the sitting members, so that whoever formed the majority could have their own proposal approved and carried. I regard factions as a phenomenon arising from that. To young people of the modern generation, a faction probably looks like a small thing.

We look at the party more broadly — in terms of its ideology and the role it performs in society — rather than at factions. A faction was one more structure built by a particular set of people, an informal structure, aimed at getting the ideas they themselves put forward supported. But as I understand it now, our young people will not be looking at anything as small as a faction. If they were to look at it that way, then there would be no possibility of reform taking place inside this party at all.

Your movement's main aim is of course to hear every member, to secure the participation of every member. So when we talk about the party's council meeting, about the congress convening — how well is that possibility, of bringing in new young people or securing every member's participation, actually reflected in the charter?

As for the charter, the Democratic Party does have a charter, and it is not all that bad, though there is much in it to correct. But the central problem that has arisen to this day has been failure to observe the charter — a problem to do with enforcing it. There are of course young people working on a draft of a new charter and developing the thinking behind it. But a charter is ultimately the document that emerges at the end; pursuing reform solely for the sake of amending the charter is not quite right either.

Let me give my own personal view. As I see it, reform has four main directions. First, ideology — what was the Democratic Party's ideology when it was first founded? In particular, what were the ideas put forward by the political forces of the time that led the democratic revolution movement of 1990? Has that been handed down to today's party, and what do the majority of today's members think about it? Second, there is the very large question of whether this party has the capacity to formulate policy. Here I am speaking of people as well: is there an institutional system at the level needed to formulate policy? And is the party building, developing and retaining the human resources capable of taking part in that work?

Third, there is the question of accountability inside the party. Are the ordinary member, the party's elected officials, and the party's leadership capable of bearing responsibility? And fourth, is there a system that makes decisions with the genuine participation of the members? I understand that reform of a political party comes about only when these four things are taken together as a whole. But this is simply my own personal position.

Looking at the Democratic Party today, this is how one can assess it. First of all, we need to look again at what the Democratic Party's ideology is. On that, for instance: civil society — how are we guaranteeing human rights, and how do we see the development of civil society? These are crucial questions in the society we live in today. Second, we have reached the point where we need to look again at our ideology on economic policy.

Why? Because lately, as the influence of revenue from the mining sector has grown, party ideology has drifted toward a system in which everyone competes to get money, and the political parties have begun doing harm to their own country. As for the actual consequence, I have my doubts about the thinking of the party's members and supporters as a whole, judging by the economic policy pursued over the past period. So we must look at this ideology anew. And within that frame the question of justice also arises: how justice exists in today's rule-of-law system, and what the party's position on it should be. Those are the things I think we have to examine.

I spoke earlier with Temuujin, also a former member, about the idea that the party lost its democratic ideology and values and so became a party unable to govern. Do you agree with him? I agree.

After the party was defeated in the 2000 election, we reorganized it — the political forces that made up the opposition of the day came together and founded today's Democratic Party. But as I see it, where we may have gone wrong is in uniting political parties that held differing ideologies. Because it became too mechanical a merger, undertaken in order to win the next election, I have the impression that the newly founded party never arrived at a shared understanding of what its genuine ideology actually was — and that impression persists to this day. Because that is how it looks when you examine the policy it pursued in the economic sphere.

So today, as a large political party with 170,000 members and supporters, some part of us ought at the very least to formulate and set out: this is our ideology. And to hold a fairly wide-ranging discussion on it, letting people's ideas compete. If we do not first create our political party's ideology, then the party's whole existence turns into the question of how to win the next election. In truth, holding state power should not be a political party's ultimate aim.

A party is a voluntary association of people who unite around an idea of how society should be organized and who have defined the means of realizing it. At the most basic level, it has to be an institution in which the ideas of the ordinary member standing at the first tier do not conflict with that ideology — only then does it carry that idea into society. Second, once it has come to state power, again: what do the citizens think? Within the frame of its ideology it must draft its platform, take their views, and implement it. It is a bridge — it cannot simply be led along by the thinking of one group, or of a minority, saying, "we have taken state power, and this is what we now think."

Yesterday I ran into a member named Üz on the street and stood talking with him, and what came across was less analysis than the sense that the party looks likely to split. You mentioned it yourself earlier — five parties, seven parties, the National United Progress Party, the Social Democrats and so on, all merged into that union; and now the sense is that we are showing signs of splitting, that a Liberal Democratic Party may be founded again. That was the information he gave. Will something like that happen? Or rather than being resolved, is it heading toward breaking apart and each going its own ideological way?

Speaking as a party member, I travelled through quite a few constituencies during the recent parliamentary election. I have also been working in the countryside and meeting people since the election. On the whole, I think the party will unite and a stronger party will emerge. But what should this union rest on to be right? If it unites on the basis of ideology, that brings a strong party back — brings it back into society.

Even now, with local elections due shortly, the party's standing in society has not become something dreadful — it was defeated in one parliamentary election. And that was a matter of the electoral system: it took few seats because of the electoral system, while more than 40% of all those who cast votes still supported the party.

But among the party's members, its supporters, the voters, all voting-age citizens of Mongolia, the belief that this should be a strong pillar of a political force has not faded, and it will not fade any time soon. So what is our duty? It is to strengthen that — the crucial matter is for us to be a political force capable of carrying people's trust ourselves and bearing the responsibility for it.

Earlier the lawyer Dembereliin Temuujin said something that drew a lot of attention. He put it this way: the MPP has no accelerator, and the DP has no brakes. In other words, there is a great deal of interest in bringing reform and development to the DP, and yet, because of the question of party financing, none of it can be resolved — and it brings in a great many things that become barriers and obstructions between them. As an economist yourself — is party financing really that important to a party's ideology and values?

On the question of party financing: where does a political party's main expenditure actually arise? Only at election time. At other times, running a capable team of fifty or a hundred people is quite enough to conduct party work at the national scale in Mongolia. But the main expenditure arises during elections. And we need to look at ourselves here. On one hand we are doing this: when party financing is discussed, we discuss it from the angle of making the financing transparent, which is of course right. But we also need to look at what the expenditure is actually being spent on. Why has so much money become necessary?

In Mongolian elections, the first reason money becomes necessary is our own law on elections. In countries other than Mongolia where democracy has developed, election campaigning is not conducted with such force and such expense. There, campaigning takes place in certain fixed locations and within fixed limits, and does not demand anything like that level of expenditure — elections are organized at low cost. That is the first point. Second, money also flows through the political party in this way: it is being spent on the party's internal elections.

Why is that happening? Because, for instance, how much expenditure does it take to become a member of the National Advisory Committee? Or what power does holding many seats on that committee bring to those people? Depending on that, we need to understand that a great deal of money is being spent on the party's internal elections. And why is so much money being spent? Let me set aside parliamentary and local elections and speak only about internal party elections — and this is not something that exists in the Democratic Party alone.

The main question is: where does it come from? It comes from the mechanism by which representatives elect representatives. Take the institution of the aimag and district party committee — what we call the mid-level party organization. The fact that members of the National Advisory Committee are elected from there is what pushes us toward a system that consumes a great deal of money. Because suppose a district party committee has around a hundred, say a hundred and five members. When we say that we will join together and cast our votes for twelve people out of that body, it becomes possible to buy those hundred and five people.

Yet members of a mid-level party body are supposed to be representatives elected from the primary level. When representatives themselves elect representatives, the result is an extremely wasteful system that consumes a great deal of money and shifts power upward, to the upper layer. So what ought we to do? First of all, an open and participatory party means this: ask the member. Do not ask the aimag and district party committee who may become a member of the National Advisory Committee. Ask the members registered at the party's primary cells, organize the election there and elect. If we have a system like that, we will no longer need to spend money on our internal elections.

And by improving the election law we need to reduce the large sums we spend on parliamentary and local elections. If we do that, the party's total money becomes a very small amount indeed. Expenditure would arise only in connection with organizing elections — the point being that there must not be a system for buying up representatives.

What should the vanguard of reform do first of all? Examine ideology, of course. Second, define through a new charter what the rights of the member at the primary level are and what their participation means. And at the very outset, what must we do? Elect the members of the aimag and district party committee from the primary level; elect the members of the National Advisory Committee from the primary level; elect the party chair from the primary level. We are establishing the principle that representatives must not elect representatives. If we do that, the party becomes the party in the true sense of the word; its internal elections will no longer require such large sums; and the possibility of buying power will disappear. That is how I see it.

The party has the groups that make its decisions — the National Advisory Committee, and then the delegates who sat at the congress. And yet, do the people currently holding leadership office in the party have any interest in this — not representatives electing representatives, but electing from the members?

Well, the majority of the committee's members do understand it. First of all, they will of course not say that the system under which they themselves were elected and came in is wrong. But what they do understand is that there has been no rotation on the National Advisory Committee over the past seven years — the people elected seven years ago are simply still there.

The question of this institution's legal competence is already being raised. The majority of the committee's members do understand it. I think the committee will take one historic decision and dissolve itself. That decision being: how the party chair is to be elected, and a working group on how the charter is to be amended — a working group will probably emerge on those two. And third, a working group connected with organizing the party congress. I think that is the last correct decision this committee is capable of making.

Beyond that, for the committee to go on representing the party and making further decisions would be too much — it would become altogether undemocratic. Considering the decisions the committee has made up to now without any rotation taking place, there is even a question as to whether these were decisions made by an institution possessing legal competence.

Legal competence is exactly what I was going to ask about. Earlier the lawyer Temuujin said that in the party's charter there is no such authority or post as an acting chair of the party — that it does not exist in the charter. There is no such thing.

Well, let me just say it straight out. I would rather not speak as though I were some formidable young person. But the more one goes on trying to solve problems by the old methods, the more this institution pushes the party's own supporters away from itself. What must we do now? I think only one choice remains: to leave behind the people who are trying to solve problems by the old methods, and move on. Here, the young people of the 1970s and 80s compete with their knowledge and ability. There is one thing we lack — political experience. But compared with other people, I do not think we are so very far behind in knowledge and ability, and I believe the young people have become capable of leading a new political force forward. So the right course is to leave behind those who intend to carry on by the old methods and move onward.

What is happening now is that to this day the committee's meeting cannot be held. The reason is that the question of who is to be made party chair is being placed above the party's reform, and that is why it cannot be held. To put it directly, the intention is to resolve the party's issues at the congress. But the National Advisory Committee cannot approve which issues are to be resolved there. Because they cannot again reach agreement over whose interest should decide the matter, they cannot even appoint and establish the working group.

And yet this party is not the property of a few people. It is a crucial institution of the democratic order, supported out of conviction throughout their entire lives by hundreds of thousands of people whose own ideas and voices could not even find a way to take part. If a few people are attempting, by their own methods, to extend their time in the party's leadership, then we will simply leave them and move on. This is a large wave.

But the committee cannot convene. Nobody knows when the congress will be called. You only said that it would be after the election — and yet when the very people at the top who make the decisions neither want it nor take any interest in it, what success can you reach? That is where there is a slight difference in the angle we are looking from. Those people are not "at the top."

No — but decisions are made by them. The committee convenes. Yes, yes. The National Advisory Committee has only three decisions it is obliged to make, as I said earlier: first, establish the working group to amend the party's charter; second, establish the working group to organize the party congress; third, the committee has the right to issue an interim procedure for the election of the party chair. And that is the historic role the committee is capable of performing.

If the committee makes those decisions and says that it has fulfilled its duty, then I see 2017 as the year in which a very large wave of internal reform passes through the party. All the aimag and district party committees will be renewed. The registration system for all members needs to be made online and digital. And it cannot go on being the case that the primary cell chair, the party cell chair, or the sum party committee chair admits or refuses to admit a person at their own discretion.

Anyone who accepts the party's ideology, accepts its charter and has paid their dues must have every possibility opened up to them to enter and compete in any part of the internal elections — only on that basis will this party move forward. And otherwise, to state my own firm personal view: having won a parliamentary election several times, or having sat as a member of parliament several times, or having worked in the party's leadership for so many years, does not mean that one stands above. As things are now, if the party proves unable to organize its congress successfully by itself, then a system will come into being for holding the party congress without these people — they cannot stand against a wave this large.

Let us close our interview here. Because I think you gave the answer to my question right at the very end: as long as the people at the top do not wish to resolve the problem, what should the young people do? I think that is the conclusion of what was said today. And so, working with us in our studio was a member of the coordinating council of the Open Democratic Party Reform Movement, the economist Chimeddorj. We have given our viewers information on a current issue. Thank you very much. Good luck. Thank you.
